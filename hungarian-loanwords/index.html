Where does this Hungarian word come from?

Hungarian is a Uralic language, but over a thousand years it has borrowed heavily from its neighbors. We train models to read a single word, letter by letter, and guess whether it is native or a German, Slavic or Turkic loanword.

The task

Four classes, one word at a time

The input is a single lemma. The output is one of four origins. These are the most frequent words of each class in our subtitle data:

native (Uralic)

Inherited from Proto-Uralic and Proto-Finno-Ugric.

tud · jön · mond · megy · lát · ember
German

Borrowed from German over many centuries of contact.

fegyver · pont · kapitány · cél · herceg · vicc
Slavic

Borrowed from neighboring Slavic languages after the Hungarians settled.

csinál · rend · dolog · baj · munka · pénz
Turkic

Mostly old West Old Turkic loans, from before the settlement.

köszön · idő · apa · kis · erő · kép

Data pipeline

From film subtitles to labeled words

We wanted words people actually say, so the vocabulary comes from Hungarian film subtitles. The labels come from etymology databases.

Read subtitles

Hungarian OpenSubtitles (OPUS v2024), streamed from a 5 GB zip in shuffled file order without unzipping.

preprocessing_updated.py

Lemmatize

Tokenize and lemmatize; drop stop words, punctuation and non-alphabetic tokens; lowercase.

HuSpaCy hu_core_news_lg

Label

Look up each lemma in a label table built from Hungarian Wiktionary, with Turkic loans from CLDF. 2,075 words fall in the four classes.

build_table.py

Clean

Keep each lemma once with its subtitle count; stop at 1,000. Remove pronouns, names and lemmatizer junk: 980 remain.

tokens_dataset_updated.csv

Evaluate

5-fold stratified cross-validation by word; 15% of each training fold held out for early stopping. Primary metric: macro-F1.

m1_updated_model.ipynb

Models

Three baselines and a character-level GRU

ModelWhat it sees
Majority classNothing: always predicts “native”
Frequency onlyLog subtitle count → logistic regression
Char n-gram LRTF-IDF of 2–4 letter sequences → class-balanced logistic regression
Char-GRUThe word as a sequence of characters → bidirectional GRU

The GRU passes a sanity check: with dropout off, it drives the training loss on 20 words (5 per class) to 0.0000 at 100% accuracy, so the model can learn and the training loop works.

Char-GRU architecture

34 characters + pad/start/end tokens · weighted cross-entropy · Adam · early stopping on validation macro-F1

class CharGRU(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dropout=0.3):
        super().__init__()
        self.emb  = nn.Embedding(37, 16, padding_idx=0)
        self.gru  = nn.GRU(16, 32, batch_first=True,
                           bidirectional=True)
        self.drop = nn.Dropout(dropout)
        self.out  = nn.Linear(2 * 32, 4)

    def forward(self, x, lengths):
        packed = pack_padded_sequence(self.emb(x), lengths,
                     batch_first=True, enforce_sorted=False)
        _, h = self.gru(packed)          # last state, both directions
        return self.out(self.drop(torch.cat([h[0], h[1]], dim=1)))

lr 3e-3 · weight decay 1e-4 · batch 32 · up to 100 epochs, patience 15 · best epochs per fold: 8, 25, 12, 26, 11

Milestone 1 results

Spelling carries real signal, but the GRU doesn't beat n-grams yet

Both character models more than quadruple the majority baseline's macro-F1. The GRU lands just below the n-gram model, a gap within the fold-to-fold variation, and its scores are much more stable across folds.
